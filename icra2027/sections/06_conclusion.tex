A pretrained policy provides a reference for useful behavior, while the
critic supplies an improvement signal. CAST makes these roles explicit:
the critic proposes action corrections, restoration opposes departure from
the frozen base, and clipping limits each target correction used to train
the residual.
Matched simulation studies show substantial success gains and earlier
improvement. The component study shows that clipping alone can fail to
preserve competence, while physical placement and stacking provide initial
evidence for refinement from recorded practice.

The results also clarify the limits of this approach. Alternative
constraints lead on individual tasks, and transport and shared-critic
multitask comparisons favor DICE-RL. Sparse reward alone does not determine
whether restoration is useful. The target cap bounds a requested correction,
whereas the distributional bound depends on the actor's actual residual;
neither guarantees successful behavior after fitting. Restoration moderates
the influence of critic guidance without establishing its accuracy.

A next step is to adapt restoration to measured local reliability and
the amount of change a task requires, alongside larger physical evaluations.
The central lesson is that a persistent behavioral reference can support
useful refinement, provided it leaves room for the corrections needed
to complete the skill.
