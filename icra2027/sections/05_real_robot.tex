\subsection{Physical Refinement: Improvement and Failure Modes}
\label{sec:exp-real-robot}

The physical study evaluates improvement beyond pretraining, with supervised
BC as an alternative to reward-based refinement. It also connects the
measured success rates to a concrete failure mode: useful intermediate
behavior followed by an unsuccessful final release.

\textbf{Robot and tasks.}
We use the seven-joint left follower arm and parallel gripper of an
OpenArm platform. Chest RealSense D435 and wrist D405 RGB images are
resized to $128\times128$ and encoded by a shared ResNet-18; the policy
also receives follower joint/gripper positions and a task-language embedding.
A flow-matching policy with a diffusion transformer (DiT) backbone uses
ten Euler steps to predict 30-step chunks of absolute joint/gripper targets.
Deployment issues commands at 15\,Hz, requests a chunk every eight periods,
limits arm-command changes to 0.6\,rad/s, and ends attempts after 20\,s.

Both tasks use a hand-made staircase: placing Jigglypuff into the adjacent
case and stacking the red cube on the purple cube. Stacking additionally
requires releasing the upper cube without disturbing its support.
Fig.~\ref{fig:real-robot} shows teleoperated demonstrations alongside
separate autonomous evaluation results.

\textbf{Refinement data and training.}
Each task uses one shared starting checkpoint. For placement, BC trains on
30 supplementary expert demonstrations; RL uses those demonstrations plus
18 reviewed attempts (11 successes, seven failures). For stacking, BC uses
12 successful attempts; RL uses all 24 reviewed attempts plus 30 original
training demonstrations. BC fine-tunes the base policy and encoder, while
both RL arms freeze them and train a residual. The BC--RL comparison
therefore changes the data, learning objective, and trainable parameters.

The training protocols specify 40 additional BC epochs and 6,000 updates
for each RL arm, with matching DICE-RL/CAST replay indices and critic
settings. Both RL arms correct the nominal eight-step executed prefix
and use a noise-independent critic because behavior latents were not logged.
Training is offline on saved experience. Only successful terminal
transitions receive reward 1; other transitions receive 0, and earlier
critic targets bootstrap estimates of future return. Image alignment is
approximate because frame timestamps were not recorded. The reported
autonomous trial counts have not yet been linked to these final training
checkpoints.

\textbf{Observed improvement.}
Each policy receives 20 autonomous attempts per task. Placement poses are
randomized on the stairs. Success requires the toy to remain fully inside
the case, or the red cube to remain on the purple cube after release and
gripper withdrawal. Paired resets and a fixed post-release observation
interval are not documented.

On placement, success rises from 55\% for the base to 80\% for BC, 90\%
for DICE-RL, and 95\% for CAST. On stacking, the corresponding rates are
35\%, 65\%, 60\%, and 70\%. All refinements show higher success than
the bases; CAST gains 40 and 35 pp, respectively. BC remains competitive
on stacking, motivating a closer look at the supervision needed to
complete the release.

\textbf{Why stacking can favor BC and CAST.}
Qualitative observations identify failure to release the gripper as the
main stacking failure mode. The robot reaches, grasps, and aligns the
cube, but does not open the gripper to complete the stack. The attempt
correctly receives a failure outcome, although the preceding motion is
useful. Refinement should correct the release while preserving this approach.

A binary terminal reward does not identify which stage caused the failure.
The critic estimates expected completion from limited experience and
bootstrapped future values; the actor relies on its action gradients to
suggest useful changes. A low value before a failed release can correctly
predict unsuccessful completion under the current policy without showing
that the preceding action was wrong. Learning an actionable correction
from that outcome is a credit-assignment problem: the learner must infer
which part of the sequence needs to change.

Success-only BC supplies direct action targets for both the approach and
the successful release. This offers a plausible explanation for its
65\% success versus DICE-RL's 60\%. DICE-RL can use failed attempts,
but its actor follows critic gradients and can relax base regularization
when the critic favors a correction. Imperfect local guidance may therefore
alter behavior that already works. CAST uses the same critic-training
procedure while restoring actions toward the base and limiting target
corrections. These controls may reduce disruption of the approach while
permitting a release correction; they do not identify release as a special
stage or improve the critic's accuracy directly.

CAST's 70\% stacking success is consistent with this explanation, but
exceeds BC by one trial and DICE-RL by two. The release observation
therefore motivates a mechanism hypothesis, rather than establishing
critic error or a release-specific repair. The observed gains over the
bases support further study; larger evaluations with verified checkpoint
linkage are needed to compare the refinements reliably.
