We organize the evaluation around three questions. First, can anchoring
preserve room for improvement beyond the pretrained policy, in simulation
and on a physical robot? Second, which aspects of the update account for
that behavior: restoration, clipping, or the critic's improvement signal?
Third, when does the balance cease to help? We establish refinement gains
on robomimic, LIBERO, and OpenArm before using ablations to examine the
mechanism and broader control experiments to identify its limits.

\subsection{Setup and Reporting}
\label{sec:exp-setup}

We use state-based robomimic manipulation~\cite{mandlekar2021robomimic},
image-based tasks from LIBERO-90~\cite{liu2023libero}, and the DeepMind
Control Suite (DMC)~\cite{tassa2018dmc}. Our controlled actor comparisons
share the pretrained checkpoint, critic-training procedure, replay recipe,
and interaction budget. The DICE-RL actor maximizes normalized critic value
with behavior-cloning regularization; CAST replaces that actor update.
Both retain the frozen observation encoder where applicable, ensemble TD
learning, multi-step returns, and demonstration replay.

Robomimic uses 300-episode evaluations. The drifting-base comparison reports
last-three-checkpoint averages over three training seeds. LIBERO uses
100 rollouts $\times$ three evaluation seeds, which should not be confused
with independent training runs. DMC reports return over 1000-step episodes,
averaged over ten evaluation episodes. Training-seed counts and unfinished
runs are marked with their tables. All improvements are relative to each
method's own base and evaluation rule.

The LIBERO recipe uses $K=16$, $\eta_0=0.05$, $\eta_{\mathrm{anc}}=1$,
$\rho=0.05$ rms, $\delta_Q=1$, and $\delta_{\mathrm{tot}}=0.15$.
Reward steps $\eta_Q\in\{1,2\}$ are shared across tasks. Robomimic and
the default DMC transfer use $\eta_Q=0.5$ and $\rho=0$; the tuned DMC
quadruped setting is disclosed below. The target-construction rule is
unchanged across these settings.

\subsection{Refinement in Simulation}
\label{sec:exp-robomimic}
\input{sections/tables/05_robomimic}

Anchoring is useful only if it allows the robot to improve beyond its
starting behavior. We test both the size of that improvement and the
interaction required to obtain it, then examine whether it persists across
visual tasks and stronger bases.

\textbf{Robomimic: gains at a fixed budget.}
The drifting-base rows of Table~\ref{tab:robomimic} isolate the actor
update. On square, CAST reaches .912--.930 from a .382 base, compared with
.861--.909 for backpropagation through the critic. It leads on each training
seed at the reported 20K update budget. Can provides a near-saturation
check: CAST reaches .978--.993 from .877, although two baseline seeds end
before the full budget and are marked accordingly.

\textbf{Sample efficiency and generator choice.}
The gain is partly one of efficiency. With longer training on drifting
square, both actors approach .97--.99 near 40K updates. On the retrained
diffusion base, they also reach similar endpoints (.965 for DICE-RL and
.959 for CAST at 496K environment steps). CAST first reaches .90 at
128/160/160K steps, compared with 224/240/256K for DICE-RL: about
one-third fewer environment steps on average. On the flow-matching base, the
value-only CAST variant reaches .934 against .831. This supports portability
of the update framework, without implying that one reward-field choice is
optimal for every generator.

\textbf{Context from other fine-tuners.}
The lower block provides external context. DIPO, QSM, DQL, IDQL, and AWR
use the DPPO authors' adapted implementations and approximately 24M
environment steps. Their stricter chunk-level success rule reads the
shared checkpoint at .348 rather than .600; we therefore use .348 for
their own-base deltas. QSM and DQL collapse in these runs, whereas AWR gains
.045. These heterogeneous systems are not a matched actor-update test.
DPPO is still training on the .600 base; a separate converged run on its
original-architecture base reaches .887 at 11.7M steps.

The next comparison asks whether the update helps beyond a single skill.
We fine-tune a separate residual and critic for each of eight challenging
LIBERO-90 tasks, starting from one multitask base and routing by task ID
at deployment. Table~\ref{tab:libero-main} reports both shared reward-step
settings rather than choosing the best setting independently per task.
On the drifting base, CAST$_2$ improves mean success from .661 to .755
($+9.4$ points), compared with .670 ($+0.9$) for the backpropagated actor.
CAST improves on the base on all eight tasks, although DICE-RL is better
on the Study-3 caddy task (.653 versus .635).

\textbf{A stronger base changes the room for improvement.}
The stronger flow-matching base leaves a smaller aggregate gain. CAST$_1$
is nearly unchanged from the base (.756 versus .753), but CAST$_2$ reaches
.777, while DICE-RL averages .750. The result therefore depends on the
reward step as well as the base. Gains are not universal: on some tasks the
unmodified flow-matching policy remains best. A critic-free GRPO comparison
is available on four tasks under the matched evaluation protocol; it is
competitive on red mug (.883 versus CAST$_1$'s .878), while CAST leads on
ketchup (.738 versus .700). We do not pool the four additional short-run
GRPO estimates into a matched eight-task mean.

\subsection{What Makes the Balance Work?}
\label{sec:exp-controls}
\input{sections/tables/05_ablations}

The refinement comparisons establish that constrained updates can improve
pretrained skills. We now separate three possible explanations: limiting
each step, resisting accumulated departure, and choosing an informative
direction of change. These are controlled interventions on the update,
rather than direct measurements of critic reliability.

\textbf{Clipping and restoration have different roles.}
Table~\ref{tab:ablations}(a) switches the two independently. Disabling
clipping removes both field and total-step caps; disabling restoration
sets both $\eta_0$ and $\eta_{\mathrm{anc}}$ to zero. Without restoration,
success is zero on every factorial task, even with clipping. Restoration
alone avoids complete collapse but is inconsistent: red mug improves from
.573 to .810, whereas ketchup falls from .610 to .387. The full pair is
the only arm above its base on all five tasks, with mean success .703
against .673 before fine-tuning. This is direct evidence that a per-step
cap and a fixed-base restoring term are not interchangeable.

\textbf{Does the form of restoration matter?}
Block (b) compares the field update with a dead-zone hinge in the DICE-RL
loss and a hard projection of the action target. The hinge performs well
on the two LIBERO tasks but reaches only .28 on square, with reported
training-seed outcomes spanning .03--.77. Hard projection is strongest on
red mug and competitive on square, but falls below the ketchup base.
CAST is consistently effective across these three tasks, rather than
uniformly best. We retain each study block's reported full-method reference
instead of pooling aggregates across ablation campaigns.

\textbf{Does improvement require the critic's gradient?}
Block (c) replaces it with kernel transport toward top-$k$ candidates or
softmax-weighted standardized $Q$-values, holding the constraint fixed.
Value-only fields lose substantially on square and ketchup. On red mug,
however, the tilted field exceeds the gradient reference in this study
(.697 versus .600). Thus the gradient is valuable but not universally
superior; its usefulness depends on the critic and task.

\textbf{How much freedom should the correction have?}
Sensitivity studies further show that an always-on pull can suppress useful
corrections on LIBERO: $\rho=0$ yields .549/.601 on mug/ketchup. Interior
radii from .02 to .20 give a broad operating range, while making the dead
zone inert still retains the weak pull. Single-seed radius and step-size
differences should be interpreted cautiously, particularly on red mug,
where substantial training-seed variation has been observed.

\subsection{Scope and Failure Cases}
\label{sec:exp-dmc}
\input{sections/tables/05_dmc}

The preceding results support anchoring in the tested manipulation
settings. They leave open whether sparse rewards alone explain its value,
and whether the base always provides a useful reference.

\textbf{DMC: reward sparsity is not sufficient to explain the gains.}
DMC tests the same residual update outside the main manipulation setting.
Bases are one-step policies distilled from SAC
experience. Table~\ref{tab:dmc} retains all six reported tasks and marks
incomplete runs rather than treating them as converged comparisons.
CAST raises dense swing-up return from 429 to 715, versus 318 for the
backpropagated actor, and dense balance from 378 to 911. Quadruped run
reports 312 before the matched budget using $\eta_Q=1$, compared with 267
for the untuned CAST arm and 283 for backpropagation. This is a tuned,
interim comparison, not a converged matched-budget advantage or evidence
of one optimal recipe.

On sparse balance, CAST reaches the maximum return of 1000 from a base of
90; on ball-in-cup catch it reaches 925 from 86. Unconstrained updates can
also improve in this setting, including under sparse reward, so sparsity
alone does not explain the manipulation failures. Conversely, dense reward
does not ensure useful unconstrained fine-tuning: the incomplete quadruped
run remains below its base. These observations are consistent with a role
for critic error, data coverage, and departure from the base, but do not
isolate their individual effects. The DMC recipe's $\rho=0$ also shows that a free dead-zone interior
is not necessary in every regime.

\textbf{When the reference or critic changes, the ordering can reverse.}
On robomimic transport, CAST reaches .594 against .912 for DICE-RL at a
matched 2.24M environment steps. This failure is consistent with anchoring
restricting a needed large correction from a weak base. With a shared
multitask LIBERO critic, best-of-16 evaluation gives .722 for DICE-RL and
.692 for CAST, reversing the per-task ordering. Together, these cases show
why neither proximity to the base nor one actor-update rule is sufficient
for reliable improvement. The useful balance depends on the starting
behavior and the critic used to guide adaptation.
