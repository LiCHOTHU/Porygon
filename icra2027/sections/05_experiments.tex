The experiments evaluate three aspects of refinement.
\textbf{H1~(useful adaptation):} CAST can improve task success or
interaction efficiency over standard residual refinement.
\textbf{H2~(persistent reference):} limiting target corrections alone
is insufficient to retain success in the tested visual manipulation tasks.
\textbf{H3~(preservation and adaptation):} neither stronger preservation
nor value-only guidance consistently improves refinement.
We first measure gains, then examine the update choices that support them,
and finally test where the manipulation findings do not transfer.

\subsection{Setup and Comparison Logic}
\label{sec:exp-setup}

\textbf{Benchmarks and controls.}
We evaluate state-based robomimic manipulation~\cite{mandlekar2021robomimic},
image-based LIBERO-90 tasks~\cite{liu2023libero}, and
DMC~\cite{tassa2018dmc}. In matched simulation studies, DICE-RL and CAST
start from the same pretrained checkpoint and share the residual
architecture, critic-training procedure, replay recipe, and interaction
budget. DICE-RL maximizes critic value with selectively applied BC
regularization; CAST denotes the same residual system with our action-target
update. The comparison therefore concerns complete actor objectives,
including their weighting of replay states. Collected experience and
learned critics can diverge as the policies change. External methods
and physical refinement use the separate protocols described below.

\textbf{Outcomes and replication.}
Task success is reported as a percentage and differences as percentage
points (pp). We measure improvement beyond the base, success relative to
DICE-RL at a fixed budget, and interactions needed to reach a common
success threshold. Robomimic uses 300-episode evaluations; drifting-base
results average the last three checkpoints separately for each of three
training seeds. LIBERO uses 100 rollouts for each of three evaluation
seeds, which measure evaluation variation rather than independent
training runs. DMC reports episode return on its original 0--1000 scale,
averaged over ten 1000-step episodes. Captions identify incomplete runs
and distinguish comparisons with different configurations.

\textbf{Update settings.}
The LIBERO recipe uses $K=16$, $\eta_0=0.05$, $\eta_{\mathrm{anc}}=1$,
$\rho=0.05$ rms, $\delta_Q=1$, and $\delta_{\mathrm{tot}}=0.15$.
CAST$_1$ and CAST$_2$ use critic step scales $\eta_Q=1$ and $2$,
respectively, shared across all eight tasks. Each column therefore
evaluates one fixed setting across tasks. Robomimic and default DMC use
$\eta_Q=0.5$ and $\rho=0$; exceptions are identified below.

\subsection{H1: Improving Success and Interaction Efficiency}
\label{sec:exp-robomimic}
\input{sections/tables/05_robomimic}

A useful reference must leave room to improve. Table~\ref{tab:robomimic}
compares refinement within each base policy, while learning trajectories
distinguish earlier improvement from a higher final success rate.

\textbf{Refining weak and strong starting policies.}
On drifting-base square, CAST reaches 91.2--93.0\% from a 38.2\% base,
exceeding DICE-RL on each of three training seeds at 20K updates.
Restoration therefore permits substantial changes beyond the base's
competence. Can starts at 87.7\%, leaving less room to improve, yet CAST
reaches 97.8--99.3\%. Two can baseline runs end before the common budget,
so this row provides a partially matched comparison. Together, these
results show improvement from both weak and strong starting policies.

\textbf{The benefit can be earlier improvement.}
With longer training on drifting square, both actors approach 97--99\%
near 40K updates. The diffusion pair likewise reaches 95.9\% for CAST
and 96.5\% for DICE-RL at 496K environment steps. Their learning
trajectories differ: CAST first reaches 90\% at 128/160/160K steps,
versus 224/240/256K for DICE-RL, requiring about 38\% fewer steps on
average. H1 is thus supported by the success attainable within a practical
interaction budget, even when final performance converges. This distinction
also matters for physical deployment, where additional experience costs time.

A value-only CAST variant reaches 93.4\% on flow-matching square, compared
with 83.1\% for DICE-RL. The interaction budget for this comparison is
unspecified, so it supports compatibility with another generator class
without establishing a speed advantage. Across base classes, absolute
scores also reflect differences in pretraining.

\textbf{External comparisons provide context.}
The lower block of Table~\ref{tab:robomimic} reports DIPO, QSM,
Diffusion-QL (DQL), IDQL, and AWR implementations adapted by the DPPO authors,
using about 24M environment steps. Their stricter chunk-level success
rule scores the base at 34.8\%, against which gains are computed. QSM
and DQL reach 0\%, while AWR improves by 4.5 pp. DPPO reaches 67.8\%
in an ongoing run from a 60.0\% base; a separate run with its original
architecture reaches 88.7\% at 11.7M steps. These outcomes show that more
interaction does not ensure useful refinement in the tested configurations.
Differences in systems, budgets, and evaluation preclude attributing their
ordering to a single CAST component.

\textbf{Visual tasks reveal both gains and exceptions.}
\label{sec:exp-libero}

LIBERO tests whether gains extend across visual tasks under shared settings.
Each task receives its own residual and critic over a shared multitask base,
with task-ID routing at deployment. On the drifting base, CAST$_2$ raises
mean success from 66.1\% to 75.5\%, a 9.4 pp gain, compared with 0.9 pp
for DICE-RL. Both CAST settings improve over the base on all eight tasks.
On red mug and ketchup, DICE-RL falls below the bases, whereas CAST$_2$
improves them by 23.9 and 11.9 pp. The gain thus includes avoiding
degradation as well as learning better behavior. DICE-RL still leads on
Study-3 caddy.

The flow-matching results show that the benefit depends on the starting
policy and update setting. CAST$_1$ changes mean success from 75.3\% to
75.6\%; CAST$_2$ reaches 77.7\%, compared with 75.0\% for DICE-RL.
Strong base skills such as juice placement have little headroom, and none
of the refinements improves that task. Limited headroom is not the only
issue: on right caddy, the 62.0\% base falls to 56.3\% under CAST$_2$.
The average gains support H1, while these exceptions show that a reference
to the base cannot ensure every proposed correction is useful.

\subsection{H2: Small Corrections Still Need a Persistent Reference}
\label{sec:exp-controls}
\input{sections/tables/05_ablations}

The component study tests the distinction between a target-step cap and
restoration toward a fixed base. Table~\ref{tab:ablations} compares no
controls, clipping only, dead-zone restoration only, and full CAST.
Both no-restoration arms set $\eta_0=\eta_{\mathrm{anc}}=0$.
The restoration-only arm also omits CAST's weak pull, so its comparison
with CAST does not isolate clipping's marginal contribution.

Both no-restoration arms reach 0\% success on all five tasks, even when
each target correction is capped. Keeping the frozen base in the
architecture is therefore insufficient to preserve its useful behavior.
Restoration changes the outcome: the restoration-only arm retains nonzero
success on every task, and full CAST exceeds each task's base. This
supports H2 in the tested setting: limiting individual target corrections
cannot substitute for a persistent reference.

Retaining some success is a weaker goal than improving the skill.
Restoration alone raises red mug from 57.3\% to 81.0\%, but lowers
ketchup from 61.0\% to 38.7\%; its five-task mean is 65.7\%, below
the 67.3\% base mean. Full CAST reaches 70.3\% and improves all five
tasks over their bases, while restoration alone still leads on red mug
and juice. These outcomes establish the practical effect of the tested
updates. They do not directly measure critic accuracy or containment of
the learned policy's outputs.

\subsection{H3: Balancing Preservation and Adaptation}
\label{sec:exp-design}
\input{sections/tables/05_update_design}

Having established the value of a persistent reference, we examine how
the constraint and guidance choices affect refinement.
Equation~\ref{eq:local-gradient} motivates a loss-space hinge: without
clipping, target regression can express the same first-order direction as
a penalized actor objective. The comparison therefore tests the implemented
updates, rather than attributing improvement to regression alone.

\textbf{A harder constraint is not consistently better.}
Table~\ref{tab:update-design}(a) compares CAST with a dead-zone hinge in
the DICE-RL objective and projection onto a base-centered target ball.
The hinge retains DICE-RL's BC filtering; the LIBERO projection arm
replaces restoration and both displacement caps. These configurations
compare complete alternatives, rather than constraint location alone.
Projection leads on square and red mug at 96.0\% and 93.0\%, but drops
to 54.0\% on ketchup, below its 61.0\% base. CAST reaches 68.0\% on
ketchup and exceeds the corresponding bases on all three tasks.

This ordering is consistent with a preservation--adaptation tradeoff:
a hard boundary can protect a useful neighborhood while excluding changes
needed for improvement. Soft restoration resists departure while permitting
such changes. The experiment does not isolate that geometric explanation,
but establishes that a stricter target boundary is not uniformly preferable.
The hinge reaches 77.0\% and 65.0\% on the two LIBERO tasks, yet only
28.0\% on square, with training-seed outcomes spanning 3--77\%.
Multiple constraint designs can support useful refinement; their relative
performance depends on the task.

\textbf{Value rankings and action gradients provide different guidance.}
Table~\ref{tab:update-design}(b) compares gradient guidance with transport
toward top-$k$ candidates and transport weighted by standardized critic
values. On square, the gradient variant reaches 91.2--93.0\%, while both
value-only variants remain below 60\%. On ketchup, both value-only
variants fall below the 61.0\% base. Ranking sampled candidates identifies
preferred actions; an action gradient proposes a local change to each
candidate. These operations need not yield equally useful corrections.

Red mug reverses the ordering: value-tilted transport reaches 69.7\%,
versus 60.0\% for gradient guidance. Thus analytic gradients are not
uniformly preferable either. The field recipes also differ in restoration
settings, so this comparison does not isolate the source of critic guidance.
It shows that both the proposed correction and the controls applied to it
matter when evaluating a refinement rule.

\textbf{The radius determines when stronger restoration begins.}
Single-training-seed sweeps examine this balance more directly. With
$\rho=0$, the strong restoring term acts on every nonzero residual,
yielding 54.9\% on mug and 60.1\% on ketchup. Positive radii from
0.02 to 0.20 give higher reported success on both tasks, consistent with
allowing local corrections before the stronger pull activates. These
exploratory sweeps do not establish an optimal radius. As
$\rho\to\infty$, the weak pull remains, so this setting still differs
from removing all restoration. Together, the comparisons support H3:
stronger preservation alone does not explain better refinement.

\subsection{Scope: When the Manipulation Findings Do Not Transfer}
\label{sec:exp-dmc}
\input{sections/tables/05_dmc}

DMC tests whether sparse rewards alone explain the manipulation failures.
Its one-step bases are distilled from Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) experience,
providing a different task family and source of pretraining data. If sparse
reward were sufficient to make unanchored refinement fail, the same failure
would occur on these sparse tasks.

\textbf{Sparse rewards do not by themselves imply collapse.}
Unconstrained refinement improves sparse balance and ball-in-cup in the
reported runs, despite its failures on sparse manipulation. CAST raises
sparse balance from 90 to 1000 return and dense swing-up from 429 to 715;
on ball-in-cup, the unconstrained interim return of 940 exceeds CAST's 925.
The difficulty illustrated by stacking is more specific than sparsity:
a delayed outcome may give little guidance about which part of an
otherwise useful skill should change. This inference also depends on
the task and available experience. The DMC comparisons do not separately
measure these factors or critic reliability.

CAST improves several DMC tasks with $\rho=0$, showing that a free interior
for the strong restoring term is not necessary everywhere. Dense rewards
also do not ensure improvement: the unconstrained quadruped run remains
below its base at the reported checkpoint. CAST's quadruped return of 312
uses a tuned $\eta_Q=1$ and is below budget, so its comparison with
DICE-RL's 283 is interim rather than a converged, matched-budget result.

\textbf{When preserving the reference can be costly.}
On robomimic transport, CAST reaches 59.4\% versus 91.2\% for DICE-RL
at matched 2.24M environment steps. This is consistent with restoration
restricting a useful correction, although the experiment does not measure
how far the best policy lies from the base. Shared-critic multitask LIBERO
also favors DICE-RL: best-of-16 success is 72.2\%, versus 69.2\% for
CAST. That DICE-RL result uses its published recipe, making this a system
comparison rather than an isolated actor intervention. These cases delimit
the benefit of anchoring: a pretrained skill provides a useful reference,
but refinement still needs informative critic guidance and sufficient
freedom to change the behavior.
