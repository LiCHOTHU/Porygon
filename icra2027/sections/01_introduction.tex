For a robot that has already learned a skill, further learning should build
on its competence rather than put that competence at risk. Generative
policies trained on demonstrations offer a useful starting point, but they
also inherit the limitations of their data. Reinforcement learning can help
them improve through interaction without additional
demonstrations~\cite{ren2024dppo,reinflow2025}. The difficulty is deciding
how much to change: adhering too closely to pretrained behavior preserves
its mistakes, while changing too freely can undo what already works.
Policy refinement thus presents a stability--plasticity tension between
retaining prior competence and adapting beyond it.

Critic-guided refinement makes this tension explicit through two imperfect
sources of guidance. The pretrained policy encodes behavior supported by
demonstrations, but cannot by itself say how to improve. A learned critic
uses subsequent experience to predict which changes may help, but its
predictions are only as reliable as the data supporting them. Optimizing
those predictions can exploit errors outside well-covered regions, raising
predicted return while reducing actual
success~\cite{fujimoto2018td3,fujimoto2019bcq}. We study refinement with
task-success rewards, where this failure is particularly costly: losing
successful behavior also reduces the positive experience available to
correct the critic. Neither the base nor the critic should therefore have
unrestricted influence over the refined policy.

This leads to our central question: \emph{how can a robot learn beyond its
prior competence without allowing an uncertain improvement signal to erase
that competence?} Residual fine-tuning offers a natural setting: retain a
frozen base and learn corrections guided by a critic~\cite{dicerl2026}.
Yet preserving the base's parameters does not preserve the behavior of the
combined policy; the residual can override the skill that remains stored
in the base. Nor is a small update sufficient protection, since many small
steps can accumulate into a large departure. Fig.~\ref{fig:problem}
illustrates this distinction. We instead use the base as a continuing
reference, with soft resistance that allows local adaptation and becomes
stronger as departures grow.

We implement this principle with \method{} (Constrained Action-Space
Transport). Building on action-target training for generative
policies~\cite{dipo2024,psenka2024qsm}, \method{} lets the critic propose
changes to sampled actions and uses the frozen base to moderate those
changes before the actor learns them. Each action is paired with its base
counterpart under the same noise. A weak restoring pull acts throughout
action space, with an additional term opposing departures beyond a chosen
radius; clipping then caps the combined target displacement. The residual
learns these detached targets by regression, without policy likelihoods or
differentiation through the frozen generator. Restoration regulates
accumulated departure, whereas clipping limits an individual requested
correction. The radius controls this balance rather than certifying critic
accuracy or guaranteeing the fitted policy's behavior.

Our evaluation asks first whether anchoring leaves enough freedom to
improve, then what makes that improvement possible. Controlled comparisons
on robomimic and LIBERO hold the base, critic-training procedure, and
interaction budget fixed, testing whether the actor update improves the
use of the same learning resources. The gains are substantial: over $50$
percentage points above the base on robomimic square and $9.4$ points
across eight LIBERO-90 tasks under per-task fine-tuning. The latter compares
with $0.9$ points for the standard DICE-RL actor. We extend this comparison
to OpenArm placement and stacking, where success-only behavioral cloning
(BC) tests whether simply imitating successful practice is sufficient.
\method{} gains $35$--$40$ points over the base and has the highest
observed success on both tasks, although its margins over the other
refinements are small. Together, these studies test improvement beyond
the base, efficiency relative to the standard actor, and the value of
critic-guided refinement on physical skills.

The ablations then distinguish improvement from its possible explanations.
On five LIBERO tasks, clipping without restoration yields zero success
throughout, whereas their combination improves on every task's base.
Comparisons with alternative constraints and critic-value fields examine
how much depends on the form of restoration and the direction of change.
Finally, scope tests challenge the assumptions behind the balance: DMC
shows that sparse reward alone does not explain the need for anchoring,
while transport and shared-critic multitask results show that a fixed-base
reference can become a liability. This progression establishes both the
value and the limits of the principle: prior competence can support
adaptation, but neither the base nor the critic is a universally reliable
guide.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A controlled study of competence-preserving policy refinement,
  distinguishing limits on individual updates from resistance to
  accumulated departure from a pretrained policy.
  \item \method{}, a likelihood-free residual update combining a bounded
  target correction with base restoration, supported by local-gradient
  and distribution-coupling analyses.
  \item Evaluations on robomimic, LIBERO, and DMC, together with a four-way
  real-robot refinement comparison on OpenArm placement and stacking.
\end{itemize}
