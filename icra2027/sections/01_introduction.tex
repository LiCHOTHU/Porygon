Learning from demonstrations gives robots a useful starting point, but a
policy that usually succeeds may still need practice to become reliable.
Reinforcement learning can provide that practice without requiring additional
demonstrations, and recent methods apply it to generative policies that
produce sequences of robot actions~\cite{ren2024dppo,reinflow2025}. The
challenge is to improve an existing skill while preserving the behavior that
already works. We study this problem when feedback is limited to task
success, so failed attempts provide little information about how to recover.

A practical approach is residual fine-tuning: freeze the pretrained policy
and learn a correction to its actions, guided by a learned
critic~\cite{dicerl2026}. The critic predicts the return of an action, and
the correction network is trained to increase that prediction. However,
critic accuracy is limited by the data available during training.
Optimizing its predictions can exploit errors outside well-covered regions
of the action space~\cite{fujimoto2018td3,fujimoto2019bcq}. The policy may
then move away from successful pretrained behavior even as its predicted
return increases. With success-only rewards, losing that behavior also
reduces the successful experience available to correct the critic.

This raises a concrete design question: \emph{should fine-tuning limit the
size of each update, or the accumulated departure from the pretrained
policy?} These are different constraints. Many small updates can move a
policy far from its starting point. A restoring term tied to a frozen
reference instead opposes that accumulated departure. Yet restoring too
strongly everywhere can suppress the corrections needed to improve. The
desired behavior is to permit local changes while resisting larger ones.

We present \method{} (Constrained Action-Space Transport), a residual
fine-tuning method that implements this principle. At each update, it samples
actions from the frozen base plus the current correction, moves them along
the critic's action gradient, and trains the correction network to reproduce
the adjusted actions. This action-improvement approach builds on prior
critic-guided training of generative policies~\cite{dipo2024,psenka2024qsm}.
\method{} combines displacement clipping with a restoring field toward the
corresponding frozen-base action. A dead zone in this field leaves small
corrections unpenalized by that term and pulls back larger departures.
The adjusted actions become fixed regression targets, so the actor update
requires neither policy likelihoods nor differentiation through the frozen
generator.

We evaluate the method on simulated manipulation tasks from LIBERO and
robomimic, using matched base policies, critic-training procedures, and
interaction budgets for the residual-actor comparison. On eight challenging
LIBERO-90 tasks, per-task fine-tuning improves mean success by $9.4$
percentage points, compared with $0.9$ points for the standard DICE-RL actor.
On robomimic square, \method{} reaches $91.2$--$93.0\%$ success from a
$38.2\%$ base, compared with $86.1$--$90.9\%$ for the standard actor at the
reported budget. These gains are conditional: staying near the base can
also limit improvement when a task requires substantially different actions.

Our five-task LIBERO ablation isolates the two constraints. Removing all
base-restoring terms causes zero success, even with step clipping.
Restoration alone avoids complete collapse but is inconsistent across tasks;
the full combination improves on every task's base. A dead-zone penalty in
the standard actor's loss also enables improvement. These results identify
restoring geometry as a useful principle shared by both actor updates.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A controlled study distinguishing per-update clipping from
  fixed-base restoration in critic-guided robot policy fine-tuning.
  \item \method{}, a likelihood-free residual update that combines
  critic-guided action targets with clipping and base-restoring fields.
  \item Manipulation experiments identifying the benefits and limits of
  anchoring, including its transfer to a standard actor's loss.
\end{itemize}
