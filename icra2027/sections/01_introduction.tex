A robot can perform most of a skill correctly and still fail the task.
In our stacking experiments, the robot often reaches, grasps, and aligns
the cube but fails to release the gripper. The attempt earns no success
reward, although much of the motion is useful. Refinement must therefore
learn from the failed outcome while retaining the behavior that brings
the robot close to success.

Generative policies learned from demonstrations provide a starting point
for this process. Reinforcement learning (RL) can improve them through
experience and task feedback~\cite{ren2024dppo,reinflow2025}, but sparse
outcomes leave the required correction implicit. A critic predicts future
reward, and its action gradients suggest changes to the policy. A low
predicted return before a failed release can correctly reflect poor eventual
completion without showing that the approach itself was wrong. With
limited experience, these gradients may offer unreliable guidance, and
optimizing learned values can exploit prediction errors and reduce actual
success~\cite{fujimoto2018td3,fujimoto2019bcq}.

The pretrained policy and critic serve complementary roles: the base
provides a reference for useful behavior, while the critic proposes how
to improve it. We ask \emph{how can refinement retain this reference while
allowing changes beyond the base's competence?} Residual learning offers
a natural architecture by adding learned corrections to a frozen
policy~\cite{dicerl2026}. Freezing the base preserves its parameters, but
the residual can still override its behavior. Limiting individual
corrections is also insufficient to limit their accumulated effect:
many small changes can eventually move far from the original skill.

We introduce \method{} (Constrained Action-Space Transport), which pairs
a limit on each target correction with a persistent restoring reference
to the frozen base. Building on action-target
training~\cite{dipo2024,psenka2024qsm} within DICE-RL's residual architecture,
CAST first asks the critic to propose a change to each sampled action.
A soft restoring field opposes departure from the corresponding base
action, and clipping caps the combined correction. The residual then
learns these explicit targets by regression. Sharing the same noise
between current and base actions gives each candidate a consistent
reference. The frozen generator needs only to supply samples, so the
update requires no policy likelihood. Restoration moderates adaptation
while allowing targets to move beyond its soft reference radius.

Our experiments examine whether this design leaves enough freedom to
improve. In matched comparisons, CAST gains over 50 percentage points
on drifting-base robomimic square. Per-task refinement across eight
LIBERO-90 tasks gains 9.4 points over the drifting base, versus 0.9 for
the standard DICE-RL actor. The benefit can also be earlier improvement:
on a shared diffusion base for square, CAST reaches 90\% success using
approximately 38\% fewer environment steps, although the two methods
eventually reach similar success rates.

Ablations then test the role of the persistent reference. In a five-task
LIBERO component study, removing all restoration leads to zero success
even when target corrections remain clipped. Restoration variants retain
nonzero success, and full CAST improves over every task's base. These
outcomes support a distinction between restricting one requested correction
and opposing accumulated departure. The mathematical analysis explains
these controls without guaranteeing that the fitted policy remains near
the base or improves task success.

Initial OpenArm evaluations connect the study to the motivating failure
mode. CAST improves placement and stacking success by 40 and 35 percentage
points over the bases. Behavioral cloning (BC) from successful stacking
attempts remains competitive with RL, consistent with the difficulty of
inferring a useful release correction from delayed outcomes. With 20 trials
per method and task, the small differences among refinements do not
establish a reliable ranking or a release-specific mechanism.

The broader results identify where the approach is less effective.
Alternative constraints lead on individual tasks, and robomimic transport
and shared-critic multitask comparisons favor DICE-RL. The DeepMind Control
Suite (DMC) also shows that sparse reward alone does not make unanchored
refinement fail. A pretrained skill is therefore a useful reference, but
its value depends on the task, the critic's guidance, and how much the
policy needs to change.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \method{}, a likelihood-free residual update that combines a
  persistent reference to pretrained behavior with clipped action targets.
  \item Analyses connecting the update to regularized actor learning and
  proximity of candidate action distributions, with explicit limits on
  what target constraints guarantee.
  \item Evaluations across generative policy classes, simulated manipulation,
  DMC, and physical robot skills, examining improvements, interaction
  efficiency, design choices, and failure cases.
\end{itemize}
