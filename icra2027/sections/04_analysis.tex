\textbf{How action corrections reach the actor.}
Target regression provides an interface for processing critic guidance
before it changes the network. A local identity shows exactly which
correction the actor receives.

\begin{proposition}
Fix the sampled states and latents, critic evaluation, and normalization
scale used to construct targets at $\theta_{\mathrm{old}}$. Let
$\Delta_i=\tilde r_i-\bar r_i$ and let
$J_i=\partial r_\theta(s,z_i)/\partial\theta$ be the residual Jacobian at
these parameters. Then
\begin{equation}
 \left.\nabla_\theta\mathcal L_{\mathrm{actor}}\right|_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}
 =-\frac{2}{KD}\mathbb E_s\!\left[\sum_i J_i^\top\Delta_i\right].
 \label{eq:local-gradient}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\textit{Proof.}
The targets are detached, so each summand contributes
$2J_i^\top(r_\theta-\tilde r_i)$. At $\theta_{\mathrm{old}}$,
$r_\theta-\tilde r_i=-\Delta_i$; averaging yields
\eqref{eq:local-gradient}.~$\square$

The transposed Jacobian $J_i^\top$ maps an action correction into its
contribution to the actor gradient. Equation~\eqref{eq:local-gradient}
shows that CAST's clips act on individual corrections \emph{before}
that mapping. This differs from clipping the actor's aggregate parameter
gradient.

For $\rho=0$ or $\rho\geq\epsilon$, the restoring displacement is the
negative gradient of the radial potential
\begin{equation}
 U(r)=\frac{\eta_0}{2}\|r\|_2^2+
       \frac{\eta_{\mathrm{anc}}}{2}
       (\|r\|_2-\sqrt D\rho)_+^2,
 \label{eq:radial-potential}
\end{equation}
with a continuous extension at zero. The two terms penalize total departure
weakly and excess departure more strongly. When both clips are inactive,
the local actor gradient is $2/D$ times that of the sampled objective
with per-action term $-\eta_Q\bar Q_\phi/q_{\mathrm{scale}}+U(r_\theta)$.
Thus regression alone does not introduce a new first-order improvement
direction. This connection motivates the loss-space hinge comparison;
it does not imply equivalence of finite updates with clipping or
DICE-RL's BC filtering.

\textbf{Why the paired reference has a distributional meaning.}
At a fixed state $s$, let $P_\theta^s$ and $P_0^s$ be the candidate action
distributions of the residual policy and frozen base, before any
value-guided selection. Assume finite second moments. Pairing actions
with the same latent $z$ defines a coupling, or joint sampling rule, whose
squared distance is $\|r_\theta(s,z)\|_2^2$. It follows that
\begin{equation}
 W_2^2(P_\theta^s,P_0^s)
 \leq \mathbb E_z\|r_\theta(s,z)\|_2^2.
 \label{eq:coupling-bound}
\end{equation}
The squared 2-Wasserstein distance $W_2^2$ is the minimum expected squared
distance over all such pairings. Evaluating one valid pairing gives the
upper bound. Residual control therefore connects to proximity of the
candidate distributions without requiring a policy likelihood.

\textbf{Scope of these statements.}
The target cap bounds a requested correction. Updating a shared network
also changes other samples' outputs, so the fitted actor need not remain
inside the same ball. Likewise, \eqref{eq:coupling-bound} depends on the
\emph{actual} residual and concerns candidates at a fixed state; it does
not bound changes after candidate selection or in the states visited by
the robot. Proximity also does not establish improvement: a nearby action
can follow an incorrect critic direction, and a useful correction may
require substantial departure. The experiments test whether these controls
preserve success while leaving sufficient room to improve.
