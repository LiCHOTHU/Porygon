\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

We consider an episodic decision process with states $s$, action chunks
$a \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times d}$ ($H$ control steps of a $d$-dimensional
action, treated as a single vector of dimension $S = Hd$), and sparse
task-success reward. A \emph{one-step generative policy} maps a state
and a noise sample to an action in a single network evaluation,
\begin{equation}
  a = \pi_0(s, z), \qquad z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I),
\end{equation}
and is pretrained on demonstrations with a drifting-model objective
\citep{deng2026drifting}: at each step, a population of policy samples
is transported by a kernel velocity field toward the data distribution,
and the network is regressed onto the transported targets. We write
$\mathcal{V}(x \mid p^{+}, p^{-})$ for this transport field acting on
query particles $x$, attracted to particles of $p^{+}$ and repelled from
particles of $p^{-}$ (\cref{sec:reward-field} recalls its exact form).

Following residual fine-tuning practice \citep{dawn2026,dicerl2026}, we
freeze the base and learn a residual head on top of it,
\begin{equation}
  a = \pi_0(s,z) + r_\theta(s,z),
  \label{eq:residual}
\end{equation}
together with an ensemble critic $Q_\phi(s, z, a)$ trained by temporal
difference with a conservative (CQL-style) penalty
\citep{kumar2020cql}. The critic training is standard and identical for
every method compared in this paper; \emph{the object of study is
solely the actor update} --- how the policy consumes the critic.

\subsection{Two structural failure modes}
\label{sec:failure-modes}

\paragraph{No likelihoods.} Policy-gradient fine-tuners
(PPO/GRPO-style, e.g.\ SimpleVLA-RL \citep{simplevlarl}) require
$\log \pi(a \mid s)$. A one-step generator defines its action
distribution implicitly through the noise map \cref{eq:residual}; its
likelihood is intractable, and unlike multi-step diffusion policies
there is no chain-MDP reformulation to fall back on
\citep{ren2024dppo,park2025fql}. The ratio-based toolbox becomes
available only after \emph{modifying the policy} --- adding fixed-variance
Gaussian noise to its output so that a surrogate log-density exists
\citep{reinflow2025} --- so the deployed policy is no longer the
deterministic one-step map that was pretrained. Our experiments include
this surrogate as a baseline; the update we develop needs no such
modification.

\paragraph{Critic exploitation through backpropagation.} The remaining
standard update --- backpropagate $-Q$ through the actor, as in DDPG
lineage methods and in DICE-RL's residual actor \citep{dicerl2026} ---
couples the \emph{size} of the policy update to the \emph{magnitude} of
the critic's action-gradient: $\nabla_\theta \mathbb{E}[-Q] =
-\nabla_a Q \cdot \partial a / \partial \theta$. But a learned critic is
trained only to \emph{rank} actions near its data. Off-manifold, its
gradient field is unconstrained --- and can be steep and wrong at the same
time \citep{fujimoto2018td3,fujimoto2019bcq,savo2025}. A steep, wrong
gradient marches the policy off the data manifold at maximum speed: the
better the critic looks to the optimizer, the worse the true policy
becomes.

\subsection{A two-regime toy experiment}
\label{sec:toy}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/toy_intuition.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{The failure, its cause, and the fix} (toy: $16$-D
  actions, critic trained on $60$ expert actions). \textbf{(a)} Under the
  standard update --- backpropagate $-Q$ through the policy --- the
  critic's opinion of its own policy rises without bound while the task
  success of that policy collapses to zero within $50$ updates. The
  optimiser is succeeding at what it was asked to do. \textbf{(b)} The
  cause: because each step's size is set by the critic's gradient, the
  policy travels enormous distances away from the actions the expert
  demonstrates ($12{,}416\times$ the expert's own spread). A
  behaviour-cloning penalty slows this but does not stop it
  ($2.8\times$); our clipped field update keeps the policy inside the
  data ($0.09\times$). \textbf{(c)} The consequence: both backpropagated
  variants end at zero success, while the bounded update retains the
  base's competence. The lesson the method is built on: what protects the
  policy is a bound on how far it may move, not a penalty competing
  against an unbounded gradient.}
  \label{fig:intuition}
\end{figure}

To see the failure cleanly we build the smallest setting that contains
it: a bandit whose expert data has two modes, a one-step policy
pretrained on that data with the drift operator, and a critic trained
\emph{only} on actions the expert actually takes. The critic is therefore
accurate on the data and unconstrained everywhere else --- the situation
every offline-trained critic is in. We then fine-tune the same base with
two update rules and vary one thing: the action dimension, which controls
how much of the space the critic has never seen.

\textbf{When the critic is reliable, follow it.} With $2$-D actions and
dense critic data, backpropagation is the best update ($0.79$ true
reward, versus $0.53$ for a value-only field update and $0.50$ for the
base). Nothing is wrong with using a critic's gradient when the critic
knows what it is talking about.

\textbf{When it is not, backpropagation is catastrophic --- and a BC
penalty does not save it.} With $16$-D actions and $60$ critic training
points, the same update drives true reward to \emph{zero} while the
critic's own estimate of that policy rises by four orders of magnitude:
the policy has walked into a region the critic scores highly and has
never seen. Adding the standard behavior-cloning penalty changes nothing
($0.00$ as well). This is the diagnostic result of the paper:
\emph{the penalty is a term in a loss, and it is competing against a
gradient of unbounded magnitude.} It loses. \Cref{fig:intuition} shows
the geometry --- the policy is not wandering, it is being pulled, quickly
and directly, toward the critic's hallucinated optimum.

\textbf{The fix that follows.} If the danger is that the update's
\emph{size} is set by the critic's gradient magnitude, then the cure is
not a stronger penalty but a bound on how far the policy may move --- and
the bound must live in action space, where the damage happens, not in
parameter space, where gradient clipping lives. Replacing the
backpropagated update with one that transports each action particle by a
\emph{clipped} velocity does exactly this: in the same $16$-D setting it
holds the base's performance ($0.61$ vs.\ base $0.65$) and keeps every
action on the data manifold, while preserving the action diversity the
base was trained for (best-of-$16$ sampling reaches $0.77$).

Two conclusions carry into the method. First, the choice is
\emph{regime-dependent}: the critic's gradient is the best signal
available when it generalizes and a liability when it does not, so a
useful update must keep it without being steered by its magnitude.
Second, what protects the policy is not which reward signal is used but
the \emph{form} of the update --- a displacement bounded pointwise in
action space, plus a restoring pull toward the base. In slogan form:
\emph{keep the compass, but bolt it to a governor}.
