\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

We consider an episodic decision process with states $s$, action chunks
$a \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times d}$ ($H$ control steps of a $d$-dimensional
action, treated as a single vector of dimension $S = Hd$), and sparse
task-success reward. A \emph{one-step generative policy} maps a state
and a noise sample to an action in a single network evaluation,
\begin{equation}
  a = \pi_0(s, z), \qquad z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I),
\end{equation}
and is pretrained on demonstrations with a drifting-model objective
\citep{deng2026drifting}: at each step, a population of policy samples
is transported by a kernel velocity field toward the data distribution,
and the network is regressed onto the transported targets. We write
$\mathcal{V}(x \mid p^{+}, p^{-})$ for this transport field acting on
query particles $x$, attracted to particles of $p^{+}$ and repelled from
particles of $p^{-}$ (\cref{sec:reward-field} recalls its exact form).

Following residual fine-tuning practice \citep{dawn2026,dicerl2026}, we
freeze the base and learn a residual head on top of it,
\begin{equation}
  a = \pi_0(s,z) + r_\theta(s,z),
  \label{eq:residual}
\end{equation}
together with an ensemble critic $Q_\phi(s, z, a)$ trained by temporal
difference with a conservative (CQL-style) penalty
\citep{kumar2020cql}. The critic training is standard and identical for
every method compared in this paper; \emph{the object of study is
solely the actor update} --- how the policy consumes the critic.

\subsection{Two structural failure modes}
\label{sec:failure-modes}

\paragraph{No likelihoods.} Policy-gradient fine-tuners
(PPO/GRPO-style, e.g.\ SimpleVLA-RL \citep{simplevlarl}) require
$\log \pi(a \mid s)$. A one-step generator defines its action
distribution implicitly through the noise map \cref{eq:residual}; its
likelihood is intractable, and unlike multi-step diffusion policies
there is no chain-MDP reformulation to fall back on
\citep{ren2024dppo,park2025fql}. Two routes recover a ratio, and
\emph{neither is available to a one-step drifting policy}. The first
\emph{modifies the policy} --- adding fixed-variance Gaussian noise to
its output so that a surrogate log-density exists \citep{reinflow2025}
--- so the deployed policy is no longer the deterministic one-step map
that was pretrained; we include this surrogate as a baseline and it is a
competitive one. The second leaves the policy untouched but requires it
to \emph{be} a flow model: FPO \citep{mcallister2025flowmatchingpolicygradients}
forms a PPO-clip ratio from differences of per-sample conditional
flow-matching losses, exploiting the CFM--ELBO relationship. That
construction needs a denoising loss defined over a flow time $\tau$; a
drifting model has no $\tau$ and is trained by field-target regression,
which carries no such ELBO link, so the surrogate does not exist for it.
On a flow-matching base FPO \emph{is} applicable and would be the natural
policy-gradient comparison --- a comparison we do not run here. The
update we develop needs no likelihood, surrogate or exact, on either base.

\paragraph{Letting the critic set the distance.} The remaining updates all
use the critic's action-gradient, and all let its magnitude decide how far
the policy moves. Backpropagating $-Q$ through the actor, as in the DDPG
lineage and in DICE-RL's residual actor \citep{dicerl2026}, does so
explicitly:
\begin{equation}
  \nabla_\theta \mathbb{E}[-Q] = -\nabla_a Q \cdot \partial a / \partial \theta ,
  \label{eq:backprop-coupling}
\end{equation}
so a steeper critic produces a larger parameter step. The
\emph{action-gradient} family --- which improves each sampled action by a
gradient step and regresses the policy onto the improved action
\citep{dipo2024,psenka2024qsm} --- avoids differentiating through the
policy, but inherits the same coupling one level up: the improved action
sits wherever $a + \alpha \nabla_a Q$ lands, so the displacement is again
proportional to the critic's steepness. Reported failures of that family
match the prediction, degrading under biased critic gradients and drifting
off the behavior distribution \citep{dprlsurvey2026}.

Why is distance the risky thing to delegate? A critic is trained to rank
actions near its data. Away from that data it is unconstrained, and can be
steep and wrong at once
\citep{fujimoto2018td3,fujimoto2019bcq,savo2025}. The two quantities then
fail asymmetrically. A slightly wrong \emph{direction} costs a slightly
wrong step. A wrongly large \emph{distance} costs the data manifold: the
policy is carried off it in one update, and the better it then scores under
the critic, the worse it actually is.

The important word is \emph{persistent}. The individual steps need not be
large; what matters is that they keep pointing the same way. The critic's
extrapolated optimum does not move as the policy approaches it, so each
update advances toward the same place and nothing returns the policy. This
distinction turns out to decide which remedy works: limiting the size of a
step does not stop a drift assembled from permitted steps, whereas a force
that returns the policy does (\cref{sec:exp-ablation}).

\subsection{A diagnostic experiment}
\label{sec:toy}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/toy_intuition.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{Which guard keeps a critic-driven update on the data?}
  Panels (a)--(b) use a diagnostic bandit: $16$-D actions, two demonstration
  modes of reward $1.0$ and $0.6$, and a critic deliberately fit to only $60$
  expert actions so that it is wrong away from them. We use it because the
  entire action space can be drawn, not because it resembles a robot task.
  \textbf{(a)} A slice of that space. Green is the \emph{true} reward, thin
  orange lines are what the critic \emph{believes}, and the two part company
  away from the data. Our update (blue) leaves the policy's actions on the
  demonstrated modes; unbounded updates exit the frame entirely.
  \textbf{(b)} The two guards, switched on and off independently. A step
  bound on its own does nothing --- $0.00$ success, and $5{,}847\times$ the
  data's spread away, no better than using no guard at all --- because a
  drift assembled from many permitted small steps is still a drift. The
  dead-zone restoring force on its own reproduces the full method exactly
  ($0.49$, $0.12\times$). This identifies the mechanism: what matters is the
  pull back toward the starting policy, not the limit on step size.
  \textbf{(c)} What that buys on real benchmarks, with base, critic, data and
  budget held fixed: robomimic square rises from $0.38$ to $0.92$, and the
  eight hardest LIBERO-90 tasks gain $+9.4$ points against $+0.9$ for the
  backpropagated actor.
  Note that in (b) no configuration exceeds the untuned base ($0.66$): the
  diagnostic is built to separate the guards, not to show improvement, which
  is the job of (c).}
  \label{fig:intuition}
\end{figure}

To see the failure cleanly we build the smallest setting that contains
it: a bandit whose expert data has two modes, a one-step policy
pretrained on that data with the drift operator, and a critic trained
\emph{only} on actions the expert actually takes. The critic is therefore
accurate on the data and unconstrained everywhere else --- the situation
every offline-trained critic is in. We then fine-tune the same base with
two update rules and vary one thing: the action dimension, which controls
how much of the space the critic has never seen.

\textbf{When the critic is reliable, follow it.} With $2$-D actions and
dense critic data, backpropagation is the best update ($0.79$ true
reward, versus $0.53$ for a value-only field update and $0.50$ for the
base). Nothing is wrong with using a critic's gradient when the critic
knows what it is talking about.

\textbf{When it is not, backpropagation is catastrophic --- and a BC
penalty does not save it.} With $16$-D actions and $60$ critic training
points, the same update drives true reward to \emph{zero} while the
critic's own estimate of that policy rises by four orders of magnitude:
the policy has walked into a region the critic scores highly and has
never seen. Adding the standard behavior-cloning penalty changes nothing
($0.00$ as well). This is the diagnostic result of the paper:
\emph{the penalty is a term in a loss, and it is competing against a
gradient of unbounded magnitude.} It loses. \Cref{fig:intuition} shows
the geometry --- the policy is not wandering, it is being pulled, quickly
and directly, toward the critic's hallucinated optimum.

\textbf{Which fix actually works.} The natural cure is to limit how far the
policy may move per update, and we tried it. It does not work: with the step
bound alone the policy still ends at zero true reward, $5{,}847\times$ the
data's own spread away --- no better than applying no guard at all. The reason
is visible in \cref{fig:intuition}(a). The critic's imagined optimum does not
recede as the policy approaches it, so every permitted step points the same
way, and a drift assembled from small permitted steps arrives just as surely
as one large one. A speed limit is not a boundary.

What works is a restoring force that is \emph{silent} within a radius $\rho$
of the base and engages beyond it. On its own, with no step bound at all, it
reproduces the full method exactly ($0.49$ true reward, $0.12\times$ the data
spread), and adding the step bound on top changes nothing
(\cref{fig:intuition}b).

Three conclusions carry into the method. First, the critic's gradient is the
best signal available where it generalizes and a liability where it does not,
so a useful update keeps the direction. Second, what protects the policy is a
force that \emph{returns} it, not one that slows it: containment is a
statement about where the policy ends up, not how fast it travels. Third --- and
this is why the dead zone rather than a plain penalty --- the restoring force
must be absent near the base, or it spends its strength opposing the critic in
the one region where the critic is reliable. A constant behaviour-cloning
penalty does exactly that, and in the same setting it also ends at $0.00$.
