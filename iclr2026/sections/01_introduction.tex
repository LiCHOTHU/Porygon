Behavior cloning has become the default way to build visuomotor robot policies,
and it works: given enough demonstrations, a cloned policy performs the task it
was shown. Its ceiling, however, is the demonstrations themselves. Teleoperated
data is expensive to collect, human operators are rarely optimal, and there is
mounting evidence that policy performance saturates as demonstrations
accumulate. Getting a deployed policy past that ceiling is now one of the
practical bottlenecks in robot learning.

Reinforcement learning is the natural complement: let the policy practice in
its environment and improve from its own experience. Applying it directly to a
modern generative policy is awkward, though, because such policies map noise to
multimodal action chunks and expose no tractable likelihood, which rules out
the PPO-style machinery that most of RL is built on. The workarounds either
inject noise into the deployed policy \citep{reinflow2025}, changing what
ships, or assume a denoising objective the policy does not have
\citep{mcallister2025flowmatchingpolicygradients}. This is why the dominant
practical recipe is \emph{residual} RL: freeze the pretrained policy, learn a
small corrective term on top of it, and score candidate actions with a learned
critic \citep{dicerl2026}. The base supplies competence and the critic supplies
improvement.

That recipe has a failure mode we believe is underappreciated, and it is
specific to the setting practitioners care about most: a policy cloned from few
demonstrations, improved online, with reward that fires only on task success.
In that regime, critic-guided fine-tuning frequently makes the policy
\emph{worse}, sometimes catastrophically so. Running three published
action-gradient fine-tuners from their authors' own code, at $125\times$ our
interaction budget, none improves its starting policy and two collapse to
exactly $0.000$ success (\cref{tab:baselines}).

The cause is a division of labour nobody chose deliberately. A critic is
trained to rank actions in the region its data covers, and it does that well.
Nothing constrains it outside that region, where it can be confidently and
steeply wrong \citep{fujimoto2018td3,fujimoto2019bcq,savo2025}. Yet the
standard update lets the critic decide not only \emph{which way} the policy
should change but --- through the magnitude of its gradient --- \emph{how far}.
Only the first is a question the critic was trained to answer. The consequences
are not symmetric: a slightly wrong direction gives a slightly wrong step,
whereas a wrongly large distance carries the policy out of the region the
critic understands, and the further out it goes the better it looks to the
critic that sent it there.

\Cref{fig:intuition} shows this with nothing hidden. We fit a critic to $25$
demonstrations lying on a narrow band of workable actions and follow its
gradient from a demonstrated action. The policy walks off the band to a point
the critic scores $1.63$ --- higher than any reward the task can return ---
where the true reward is exactly $0.000$. Repeat with $700$ demonstrations and
the effect vanishes: the critic has seen enough to know the band. The failure is
not a property of critics in general but of post-training from few
demonstrations. Sparse reward is what makes it terminal rather than temporary:
under dense reward a policy that wanders still receives graded feedback and can
recover, and we verify that the same unconstrained update survives there; under
success-only reward, a policy that has stopped succeeding sees nothing but
zeros and has no signal with which to return.

We propose to split the two roles apart, in a method we call \method{}
(\emph{Constrained Action-Space Transport}). The critic keeps the job it is good at
--- choosing the direction in which each action should change --- and the
algorithm takes the job the critic was never trained for: deciding how far the
policy may end up from the policy we started with. Concretely, candidate
actions are drawn from the frozen base, displaced along the critic's gradient,
pulled back whenever they stray beyond a fixed radius of their starting action,
and regressed into a lightweight residual head. Nothing else in the pipeline
changes, so the actor update is the only variable in every comparison we
report.

Our contributions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A diagnosis of why critic-guided post-training fails from few
  demonstrations} (\cref{sec:toy}, \cref{fig:intuition}). We show, on a fitted
  critic rather than by argument, that the failure is an extrapolation problem
  with a sharp data threshold: at $25$ demonstrations the critic reports a
  value above anything the task can return at an action whose true reward is
  zero, and at $700$ the effect disappears. We further show that sparse reward
  is what makes the failure permanent, by exhibiting the same unconstrained
  update surviving under dense reward.

  \item \textbf{An actor update that separates direction from distance}
  (\cref{sec:method}). Improving actions along $\nabla_a Q$ and regressing the
  policy onto them is an established family \citep{dipo2024,psenka2024qsm,
  dprlsurvey2026}; we adopt that form and add what it lacks --- a bound, placed
  in action space where the harm occurs rather than in the loss, where a
  penalty must out-shout a gradient of unbounded size and loses ($0.549$ and
  $0.601$ against bases of $0.573$ and $0.610$). Two further choices separate
  our instantiation: targets come from \emph{freshly drawn} particles off a
  frozen base rather than buffer actions mutated in place, so stored
  transitions remain consistent with the dynamics that produced them, and the
  update needs no likelihood.

  \item \textbf{Evidence that the update rule is what wins, under a strict
  control} (\cref{sec:experiments}). Holding base, critic, data and budget
  fixed and varying only the actor update, our rule gains $+9.4$ points on the
  eight hardest LIBERO-90 tasks where backpropagating $-Q$ gains $+0.9$, lifts
  robomimic square from $0.382$ to $0.91$--$0.93$, and transfers unchanged
  across generator classes. Two controls close the obvious alternative
  explanations: sweeping each method's step size an order of magnitude leaves
  the two in non-overlapping bands, so the gap is not tuning; and deleting only
  the critic's gradient direction collapses performance below base, so the gain
  is not mere caution.

  \item \textbf{An ablation that overturns the intuitive explanation}
  (\cref{sec:exp-constraint}). The natural reading of our own method is that it
  works by limiting step size. It does not. With the step clip alone success is
  exactly $0.000$ --- identical to using no constraint --- because a drift
  assembled from many individually permitted steps arrives just as surely as
  one large one. What makes the update viable is the term anchored to the
  frozen base. The effective constraint is a tether to a fixed reference, not a
  speed limit, and we report this because it contradicts what we expected to
  find.
\end{itemize}
