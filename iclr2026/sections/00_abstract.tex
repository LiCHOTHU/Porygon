% (Residual Off-Policy RL, arXiv 2509.19301; DPPO, arXiv 2409.00588):

% Kept short and jargon-free; ends on the line worth remembering.
Behavior cloning has made visuomotor robot policies easy to train, but their
performance is bounded by the demonstrations they imitate. Reinforcement
learning offers a way past that bound by letting the policy practice against a
learned critic, yet in the regime that matters in practice --- a policy cloned
from few demonstrations, improved online, rewarded only when the task succeeds
--- critic-guided fine-tuning is unreliable and frequently destroys the policy
it was meant to improve. The cause is that a critic is accurate only on the
narrow set of actions it was trained on, while the standard update lets that
critic determine both the direction and the magnitude of every policy change.
The policy is therefore pushed outside the region the critic understands,
where it stops succeeding and, under sparse reward, receives no signal that
would guide it back.

We present a fine-tuning rule that separates these two roles: the critic
chooses the direction of the update, and the algorithm chooses the distance.
Candidate actions are drawn from the frozen pretrained policy, displaced along
the critic's gradient, pulled back whenever they stray beyond a fixed radius of
the action they started from, and regressed into a lightweight residual head.
The critic, replay data and interaction budget are unchanged from the
strongest published residual fine-tuner, so the actor update is the only
variable.

Across two benchmarks this yields $+9.4$ points on the eight hardest LIBERO-90
tasks where backpropagating the critic yields $+0.9$, and raises robomimic
square from $0.382$ to $0.91$--$0.93$, while published methods that let the
critic set the distance fail to improve their base and in two cases collapse to
zero. Ablating our own components isolates why: bounding how far the policy
moves per step changes nothing, and the component that matters is the one
holding the policy near where it started. The effective constraint is a leash
to a fixed anchor, not a speed limit.
