Our experiments answer four questions. \textbf{Q1:} Given the \emph{same}
critic, data and budget, does this actor update beat backpropagating $-Q$?
\textbf{Q2:} Can a one-step drifting base fine-tuned this way match the
stronger flow-matching pipeline? \textbf{Q3:} \emph{Which component is
responsible} --- the restoring anchor, or the bound on step size?
\textbf{Q4:} When should the reward field use the critic's gradient rather
than only its values?

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:exp-setup}

\textbf{Benchmarks.} (i) \emph{robomimic} can and square (state-based;
300-episode evaluations, average of the last three checkpoints; 3 seeds),
run inside the official DICE-RL harness with only the actor update
replaced --- critic, replay, demonstrations, and evaluation protocol are
byte-identical to the baseline. (ii) The \emph{eight hardest tasks of
LIBERO-90} (multitask drifting base; 100 RL iterations; final numbers use
powered evaluation: 100 rollouts $\times$ 3 evaluation seeds).
\todo{appendix table: env details, horizons, demo counts, budgets.}

\textbf{Compared methods.} Each comparison is chosen to isolate one of the
three questions; every method on the drifting base shares the residual
parameterization, critic, data, and budget, so only the actor update
varies.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{\method{}}: \cref{eq:update} with the gradient reward
  field $V_Q^{\mathrm{grad}}$.
  \item \textbf{Backprop actor} (the controlled comparison for
  \textbf{Q1}): the DICE-RL residual actor \citep{dicerl2026} ---
  backpropagated $-Q$ with a filtered BC penalty --- run on \emph{our}
  drifting base. It consumes the identical critic through the identical
  residual head, so any gap to \method{} is attributable to the update
  \emph{form}, not to the base, the reward signal, or the data.
  \item \textbf{FM + DICE-RL} (the external reference for \textbf{Q2}):
  the unmodified state-of-the-art pipeline on its own flow-matching base
  \citep{dicerl2026}. It answers whether the one-step base plus \method{}
  competes with the strongest published system, not merely with its own
  control.
  \item \textbf{\method{} (top-$k$)} and \textbf{\method{} (tilted)}
  (resolution ablations for \textbf{Q3}, not baselines): the same update
  with the reward field built from the critic's \emph{rankings}
  (transport toward the top-$k$ particles) or its \emph{soft values}
  ($\exp(Q/\tau)$-weighted self-attraction). Each deletes the critic's
  gradient direction while changing nothing else, measuring how much of
  \method{}'s gain flows through that one channel --- and, in the
  weak-critic LIBERO regime, whether the gradient is worth consuming at
  all.
\end{itemize}
Hyperparameters are \emph{family-shared} across \method{} and its two
resolution variants, so comparisons isolate the update rule rather than
tuning effort. One recipe is used per benchmark and never per task:
$\delta_{\mathrm{tot}}{=}0.15$, $\eta_{\mathrm{anc}}{=}1.0$, $\tau{=}1$,
$k{=}4$ throughout, with reward step $\eta_Q{=}0.5$ on robomimic and
$\eta_Q{=}1.0$ on LIBERO, and a dead-zone radius $\rho{=}0.05$ in
dimension-independent rms units on LIBERO (\cref{sec:exp-ablation} shows why
the units matter).

\Cref{tab:robomimic} gives the per-seed results, and \cref{fig:curves}

\method{} reaches $0.978$--$0.993$ from a base at $0.877$, the

trails slightly at $0.940$--$0.970$ --- the can task chiefly certifies
that the field update preserves (and slightly extends) the base's
competence at the ceiling. The discriminative task is square, where the drifting base solves
only $0.382$ of episodes; \method{} adds $55$ points, reaching
$0.91$--$0.93$ on every seed. The backprop actor --- same base,
same critic, same data, same budget --- reaches $0.86$--$0.91$
(\textbf{Q1}): given the identical reward signal, the field-target form
extracts strictly more progress at the matched budget. Against the
flow-matching pipeline, \method{} reaches parity on both tasks
($0.91$--$0.93$ vs.\ $0.92$--$0.93$ on square; $0.99$ vs.\ $0.99$ on can)
--- but from a base less than half as strong on square (\textbf{Q2}): the
safer update recovers the headroom the weaker base gives up.

\emph{Why does the form win?} The anchor decouples two jobs that a
behaviour-cloning penalty must do with one knob. Inside the dead zone the
critic is followed without opposition; outside it the policy is returned.
That is what lets \method{} tolerate a reward step
($\eta_Q{=}0.5$) far above what the backprop actor can survive,
and simply takes more, safer steps in the directions where the critic is
consistent. The backprop actor must instead keep its effective step small
enough for its \emph{worst} critic errors, and it pays in speed:
\method{} reaches $0.91$ on every seed by $15$K steps --- a level the
backprop actor needs another $5$--$10$K steps to reach. Its
matched-budget endpoint trails \method{}'s on every seed, and the gap
closes only near $40$K, where both converge to $0.97$--$0.99$. The form's advantage on this benchmark is therefore a
faster, never-worse path to the same asymptote --- exactly what a
bounded-step update should buy \todo{quantify further with the
stability-window ablation (\cref{sec:exp-ablation}, iii)}.

The dial answers \textbf{Q3} in its first regime: with a critic that
generalizes (robomimic critics see dense, single-task data), the value-only
reward fields preserve can ($0.91$--$0.95$) but collapse on square
($0.53$--$0.58$ versus \method{}'s $0.91$--$0.93$), despite
verified-healthy residuals and longer training. Deleting the gradient
direction throws away exactly the
signal that solves the harder task --- the real-benchmark mirror of the
toy's easy regime, where the compass wins.

\subsection{LIBERO hard-8: the weak-critic regime}
\label{sec:exp-libero}

\emph{routing system}: one frozen multitask drifting base, one small
per-task residual head and critic fine-tuned by \method{} on each task's
own interactions, and task-ID routing at deployment --- the architecture
every published LIBERO RL fine-tuner effectively uses. One recipe is
shared across all tasks with no per-task tuning ($\eta_Q{=}1.0$,
dead-zone anchor with a dimension-independent rms radius $\rho{=}0.05$,
$3$-step returns, $40$ iterations of $16$ episodes).

\Cref{tab:libero-single} gives the verdict across all eight tasks:
\method{} gains $+3.9$ to $+23.9$ points per task (mean $+9.4$ at
$\eta_Q{=}2$, $+7.7$ at $\eta_Q{=}1$), improving on the base for
\emph{every} task at both settings --- on the highest-headroom task it reaches $0.781$,
above the best previously recorded result on that task by any method ---
while the backpropagated actor consuming the \emph{same} per-task
critics averages $+0.9$, going \emph{below base} on the two
highest-headroom tasks. The robomimic ordering (\textbf{Q1}) transfers
intact to the harder benchmark: identical reward signal, and only the
field-target form converts it into gains without ever falling below the
base. Two implementation findings were load-bearing and are ablated in
\cref{sec:exp-ablation}: the anchor's dead zone must be defined in
dimension-independent rms units (the vector-norm form silently
re-activates the anchor at LIBERO's $S{=}112$ chunk dimension and
freezes the residual), and the weaker critic gradient
($\|\nabla_a Q\|$ roughly $4\times$ smaller than robomimic's) requires
the doubled reward step the anchor makes safe. Sweeping the reward step across all eight tasks confirms
this at the table level: $\eta_Q{=}2$ improves the mean from $0.738$ to
$0.755$ and helps on six of eight tasks (the two exceptions, $-0.014$ and
$-0.009$, are inside the seed spread), and the benefit is
\emph{uncorrelated} with the critic's action-gradient magnitude --- the
task with the deadest critic (t81, $\|\nabla_a Q\|\!=\!0.007$) gains
$+2.8$, as much as the strongest-gradient tasks. The reward step therefore
acts mainly by reducing the anchor's drag rather than by exploiting more
first-order signal, and the trust region is what keeps the larger step
safe: no setting on any task falls below its base.

The flow-matching rows settle \textbf{Q1} independently of the base. On
its own, stronger base the state-of-the-art pipeline extracts essentially
nothing per task: FM + DICE-RL scores $-0.8$, $-0.2$, $+2.2$ and $-2.8$

the same near-zero extraction its actor shows on the drifting base
($+0.9$). Swapping only the actor update helps on three of the four tasks:
\method{} on the \emph{identical} flow-matching base and budget beats
FM + DICE-RL by $+5.6$ (t32), $+6.9$ (t65) and $+1.6$ (t75), reaching
$0.738$ and $0.878$ on the first two --- $+4.8$ and $+9.1$ over that base.
The exception is instructive: on t53 the flow-matching base already scores
$0.917$ and \emph{neither} update improves it ($-1.8$ for \method{},
$-0.2$ for DICE-RL). Indeed the never-below-base behaviour \method{}
shows on all eight drifting-base tasks does not carry over to this base:
on t53 and t75 it ends $2.0$ and $1.2$ points below its starting point.
Fine-tuning a base that is already near its ceiling is a different problem
from lifting a weak one, and our trust region is calibrated for the
latter.

Adding the critic-free policy-gradient baseline at the same budget
completes the picture. On the one task with real headroom from the
flow-matching base, GRPO and \method{} extract essentially the same
amount ($0.883$ and $0.878$ on t65, both $\approx\!+9.5$) while the
backpropagated actor manages $+2.2$; on t32 \method{} leads ($0.738$ vs.\
$0.700$); on the two tasks where no method gains, all three sit together.
The separation on this benchmark is therefore not \method{} against the
world but \emph{update form} against backpropagation-through-the-critic:
the field update matches what a policy-gradient method extracts, without
requiring the noise-injection surrogate that makes likelihood ratios
available for a one-step generator in the first place. The field-target form,
not the base and not the reward signal, is what converts critic signal
into success. It also puts the \textbf{Q2} comparison in perspective:
\method{} on the weak one-step base reaches $0.781$ on t65 against the
flow-matching pipeline's $0.809$ --- within three points, from a base
$21$ points lower --- and \emph{exceeds} it on t75 ($0.657$ vs.\ $0.592$)
and on t32 once the reward step is matched to that task's stronger critic
gradient ($0.729$ vs.\ $0.682$).

\Cref{tab:libero-ablation} isolates each component. Three findings
matter. First, \emph{the anchor geometry is load-bearing}: removing the
dead zone entirely ($\rho{=}0$) drops t65 to $0.549$, \emph{below} the
un-tuned base --- the un-gated restoring pull erases the residual, the
quantitative version of the toy's ``penalties lose to fields'' lesson ---
and measuring the dead-zone radius in vector-norm rather than
dimension-independent units costs $11.9$ points, because at LIBERO's
$S{=}112$ chunk dimension the base residual already exceeds $\rho$ at
initialisation, so the anchor is active from the first update.

Second, and most decisively, \emph{deleting the critic's gradient
direction is fatal, and the effect replicates}. On t65 top-$k$ transport
falls to $0.577$ and tilted to $0.697$; on t32 the same two substitutions
collapse to $0.341$ and $0.378$ --- not merely below the full recipe but
far \emph{below the un-tuned base} ($0.610$), a $34$- and $31$-point
deficit. Two tasks, two bases (\cref{tab:robomimic} shows the same
ordering on robomimic square), one conclusion: the ranking information a
critic can supply is not a substitute for its gradient, and \textbf{Q3}
is answered the same way wherever we ask it. This is what separates the
method from value-only transport such as critic-ranked top-$K$ drift.

Third, the remaining knobs are \emph{task-dependent, and in a way that
tracks the critic rather than the task's difficulty}. On t32, whose
critic carries a strong action-gradient
($\|\nabla_a Q\|\!\approx\!0.55$), $3$-step returns are worth $17$ points
and a doubled reward step $4.5$ ($0.729$), while widening the movement
bound alone ($\delta_{\mathrm{tot}}{=}0.25$) does nothing ($+0.5$): this
task wants more \emph{reward} signal, not more room to move. On t65,
whose gradient is an order of magnitude weaker ($\approx\!0.035$),
the pattern inverts --- $3$-step returns and the doubled step are within
noise, but widening the trust region is worth $+9.3$ points ($0.874$),
the single largest positive ablation we measure. We report this as a
regime difference rather than a tuned optimum: it is one seed, and t65
carries a $\pm 9$-point seed spread (\cref{sec:limitations}), so the
direction is trustworthy and the magnitude is not. What a bounded-step
field update needs is therefore not one universal setting but one matched
to what the critic actually carries --- and the trust region is what makes
raising either knob safe to attempt.

\paragraph{Does the size of the dead zone matter?}
\cref{tab:rho-sweep} sweeps $\rho$ over two orders of magnitude. Every
interior setting lands between $0.771$ and $0.863$ on t65 and between $0.671$
and $0.734$ on t32 --- a spread no larger than this benchmark's seed-to-seed
variation --- while the $\rho{=}0$ endpoint, which is exactly an always-on
behaviour-cloning penalty, falls to roughly base level on both tasks. Removing
the anchor entirely ($\rho{\to}\infty$) is \emph{not} similarly fatal, because
the step bound is still present. Removing both is: performance is exactly
$0.000$ on both tasks. We read this as follows. Some constraint tying the
policy to its base is necessary, and its absence is catastrophic rather than
merely costly. Beyond that, at this budget we cannot distinguish between the
available constraints, and we do not claim a preferred radius; $\rho{=}0.05$
is a choice inside a wide flat region, not an optimum.

\paragraph{How often does the bound actually bind?}
It would be easy to over-read our own mechanism, so we measured it. The clip
in \cref{eq:update} only ever \emph{reduces} a displacement, so whenever a
particle's proposed motion is already shorter than $\delta_{\mathrm{tot}}$ the
update is exactly the unbounded one, and the critic is still setting the
distance for that particle. In the \cref{sec:toy} setting, at our operating
point ($\delta_{\mathrm{tot}}{=}0.15$, $\eta_Q{=}1$), the clip is active on
$17.3\%$ of sampled actions, and the median proposed displacement is only
$0.12\,\delta_{\mathrm{tot}}$. Tightening the cap to $0.05$ raises the
binding rate to $26.3\%$; loosening it to $1.0$ drops it to $4.8\%$.

These numbers are worth stating because they rule out the reading we
initially favoured. If the cap were the mechanism, it would have to be doing
its work through the $17\%$ of proposals it truncates --- yet removing the cap
entirely, while keeping the restoring force, leaves the method unchanged, and
keeping the cap while removing the restoring force destroys it
(\cref{fig:intuition}b). The cap is a numerical safeguard. What holds the
policy on the data is the anchor.

\paragraph{Is the gap just a better step size?}
The most economical explanation of \cref{tab:libero-single} would be that
we simply tuned our update's step better than the baseline's. We tested
that directly on t65 by sweeping each method's own step-size knob under
the identical evaluation protocol, and the explanation fails. Sweeping
the backpropagated actor's learning rate over a $10\times$ range gives
$0.550$ ($10^{-4}$), $0.548$ ($3\!\times\!10^{-4}$) and $0.578$
($10^{-3}$) --- every setting at or below the un-tuned base ($0.573$).
Sweeping our reward step over an $8\times$ range gives $0.775$, $0.817$,
$0.908$ and $0.750$ for $\eta_Q \in \{1,2,4,8\}$ --- every setting
$18$--$33$ points \emph{above} that same base. The two bands do not
overlap at any step size either method was given. A larger step does not
convert the backpropagated update into the field update; the difference
is the form, and it is what makes a large step survivable at all. (Both
sweeps are single-seed and the within-band ordering should not be read as
a tuned optimum --- the $\eta_Q{=}4$ peak sits close to this task's seed
spread. The band \emph{separation}, at $18$ points minimum, does not.)
