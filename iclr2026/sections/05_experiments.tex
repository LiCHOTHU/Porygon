Our experiments answer three questions. \textbf{Q1:} Does the field-target
\emph{form} beat backpropagation given the \emph{same} reward signal and
budget? \textbf{Q2:} Can a one-step drifting base fine-tuned with \method{}
overtake the stronger flow-matching pipeline? \textbf{Q3:} When should the
reward field use the critic's gradient versus only its values --- does the
regime picture of \cref{sec:toy} hold on real benchmarks?

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:exp-setup}

\textbf{Benchmarks.} (i) \emph{robomimic} can and square (state-based;
300-episode evaluations, average of the last three checkpoints; 3 seeds),
run inside the official DICE-RL harness with only the actor update
replaced --- critic, replay, demonstrations, and evaluation protocol are
byte-identical to the baseline. (ii) The \emph{eight hardest tasks of
LIBERO-90} (multitask drifting base; 100 RL iterations; final numbers use
powered evaluation: 100 rollouts $\times$ 3 evaluation seeds).
\todo{appendix table: env details, horizons, demo counts, budgets.}

\textbf{Compared methods.} Each comparison is chosen to isolate one of the
three questions; every method on the drifting base shares the residual
parameterization, critic, data, and budget, so only the actor update
varies.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{\method{}}: \cref{eq:update} with the gradient reward
  field $V_Q^{\mathrm{grad}}$.
  \item \textbf{Backprop actor} (the controlled comparison for
  \textbf{Q1}): the DICE-RL residual actor \citep{dicerl2026} ---
  backpropagated $-Q$ with a filtered BC penalty --- run on \emph{our}
  drifting base. It consumes the identical critic through the identical
  residual head, so any gap to \method{} is attributable to the update
  \emph{form}, not to the base, the reward signal, or the data.
  \item \textbf{FM + DICE-RL} (the external reference for \textbf{Q2}):
  the unmodified state-of-the-art pipeline on its own flow-matching base
  \citep{dicerl2026}. It answers whether the one-step base plus \method{}
  competes with the strongest published system, not merely with its own
  control.
  \item \textbf{\method{} (top-$k$)} and \textbf{\method{} (tilted)}
  (resolution ablations for \textbf{Q3}, not baselines): the same update
  with the reward field built from the critic's \emph{rankings}
  (transport toward the top-$k$ particles) or its \emph{soft values}
  ($\exp(Q/\tau)$-weighted self-attraction). Each deletes the critic's
  gradient direction while changing nothing else, measuring how much of
  \method{}'s gain flows through that one channel --- and, in the
  weak-critic LIBERO regime, whether the gradient is worth consuming at
  all.
\end{itemize}
Hyperparameters are \emph{family-shared} across \method{} and its two
resolution variants ($\eta_Q{=}0.5$,
$\delta_{\mathrm{tot}}{=}0.15$, $\eta_{\mathrm{anc}}{=}1.0$, $\tau{=}1$,
$k{=}4$; dead zone $\rho{=}0.05$ on LIBERO) so that comparisons isolate
the update rule rather than tuning effort.

\Cref{tab:robomimic} gives the per-seed results, and \cref{fig:curves}
the corresponding learning curves. On can, fine-tuning saturates the task:
\method{} reaches $0.988$--$0.993$ from a base at $0.877$, and the
flow-matching pipeline reaches $0.986$ --- the can task chiefly certifies
that the field update preserves (and slightly extends) the base's
competence at the ceiling. The discriminative task is square, where the drifting base solves
only $0.382$ of episodes; \method{} adds $55$ points, reaching
$0.91$--$0.93$ on every seed. The backprop actor --- same base,
same critic, same data, same budget --- reaches $0.87$--$0.91$
(\textbf{Q1}): given the identical reward signal, the field-target form
extracts strictly more progress at the matched budget. Against the
flow-matching pipeline, \method{} reaches parity on both tasks
($0.91$--$0.93$ vs.\ $0.92$--$0.93$ on square; $0.99$ vs.\ $0.99$ on can)
--- but from a base less than half as strong on square (\textbf{Q2}): the
safer update recovers the headroom the weaker base gives up.

\emph{Why does the form win?} The trust region converts critic error
from a stability threat into bounded noise: with policy motion capped at
$\delta_{\mathrm{tot}}$ per update, \method{} tolerates a reward step
($\eta_Q{=}0.5$) far above what the backprop actor can survive,
and simply takes more, safer steps in the directions where the critic is
consistent. The backprop actor must instead keep its effective step small
enough for its \emph{worst} critic errors, and it pays in speed:
\method{} reaches $0.91$ on every seed by $15$K steps --- a level the
backprop actor needs another $5$--$10$K steps to reach. Its
matched-budget endpoint trails \method{}'s on every seed, and the gap
closes only near $40$K, where both converge to $0.97$--$0.99$. The form's advantage on this benchmark is therefore a
faster, never-worse path to the same asymptote --- exactly what a
bounded-step update should buy \todo{quantify further with the
stability-window ablation (\cref{sec:exp-ablation}, iii)}.

The dial answers \textbf{Q3} in its first regime: with a critic that
generalizes (robomimic critics see dense, single-task data), the value-only
reward fields preserve can ($0.91$--$0.95$) but collapse on square
($0.53$--$0.58$ versus \method{}'s $0.91$--$0.93$), despite
verified-healthy residuals and longer training. Deleting the gradient
direction throws away exactly the
signal that solves the harder task --- the real-benchmark mirror of the
toy's easy regime, where the compass wins.

\label{sec:exp-libero}

diagnostics show its action-gradient carries far less signal
